# 03 Many assets: the simulator for a universe

[![Open In Colab](https://colab.research.google.com/assets/colab-badge.svg)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/mamadouyamar/CVineMarketGen/blob/main/examples/03_many_assets_factors.ipynb)

**The problem**

You manage a universe of many assets and you need scenarios for it: synthetic
months of returns, one column per asset, many thousands of rows, that you can
feed to an optimizer, a risk engine or a pricing model. The scenarios must
reproduce what you believe about each asset, its mean, its volatility, the
shape of its tails, and how the assets move together, in calm months and in
bad ones.

Three things make this hard when the universe is large.

1. **Too many numbers to estimate.** Sixty-eight assets have 2,278 correlations
   and 272 moments. From 230 months of history these cannot be estimated one by
   one; a correlation matrix of that size estimated from that sample is mostly
   noise.
2. **Not every asset has a history.** A new fund, a private allocation, a
   product about to be launched has no return series, only a view on what it
   earns, how much it moves and what it depends on. It must still be in the
   scenarios.
3. **Views are about drivers, not assets.** An analyst's view is on equities,
   on rates, on credit spreads, on inflation. Written on 68 assets one by one, a
   view is inconsistent by construction.

Notebooks 01 and 02 built a generator for six assets from their moments and
their dependence. This notebook takes that generator to a universe.
""")
md(r"""
## The route: a few drivers, then a map to the assets

The simulator does not draw the assets. It draws a small number of common
drivers, the factors, and maps each simulated factor month to every asset:

$$
r_j = \alpha_j + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{jk}\, f_k + e_j .
$$

The factors $f_k$ are drawn together by the generator of notebook 02, which
reproduces their moments, their correlations and their tail dependence. The
betas $\beta_{jk}$ say how much of each factor asset $j$ carries. The residual
$e_j$ is what remains of the asset once the factors are removed, drawn from
its own distribution and independent of the other assets.

This answers the three difficulties. Seven factors have 21 correlations and 28
moments, which 230 months can estimate; the 2,278 asset correlations follow
from the betas. An asset without a history is simulated from its exposures and
a residual sized to its volatility. A view is stated on a factor and reaches
every asset through the betas.

**The process.** The four steps of notebooks 01 and 02, applied to the
factors and then carried to the assets:

1. *Targets*: what the factors must reproduce.
2. *Fit*: the generator on the factors, and the betas and residual of each asset.
3. *Check*: the scenarios against every input.
4. *Use*: scenarios and paths out.

What you have decides how each step is done. For every asset and every factor
there are four possible pieces of knowledge: a return history, moments,
exposures, correlations. The sections that follow take the steps in order and,
at each one, go from the case where you have the least to the case where you
have the most.
""")

## What you have

The first question the simulator asks. Before any step, list what you know
about each asset and each factor you want in the scenarios. Four kinds of
knowledge, any combination:

| Knowledge | Asset | Factor |
|---|---|---|
| a return history | yes / no | yes / no |
| moments: mean, volatility, and if you have them skewness and kurtosis | yes / no | yes / no |
| exposures to the factors: which factors it may load on (the a-priori table), and/or the values of some betas, or a correlation with a factor | yes / no | not applicable |
| correlations | with a named asset | with some factors |

**The workbook.** This knowledge goes in one Excel workbook, six sheets, that
the simulator reads in one call. The Python-fluent analyst builds the same six
tables in code.

| Sheet | Columns | What a row says |
|---|---|---|
| `assets` | ticker, tag, mean, vol, skew, kurt | an asset in the scenarios, its asset class, and any moment you impose (annual) |
| `tags` | tag, one column per factor | the a-priori table: for each asset class, empty = does not load, `fit` = estimate the beta, a number = the beta |
| `exposures` | ticker, factor, beta, corr | one asset departing from its tag on one factor: a beta, or a correlation with the factor |
| `pairs` | ticker_1, ticker_2, corr | a correlation you impose between two assets |
| `factors` | factor, mean, vol, skew, kurt | a factor and any moment you impose (annual); a factor with no history must be here with mean and vol |
| `factor_corr` | factor_1, factor_2, corr | a correlation you impose between two factors, or give for a factor without a history |

""")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ step 2b: the rule
md(r"""
## Step 2b: the map from factors to assets

**The object.** For each asset, the equation of the route: return = alpha +
betas times factors + residual. The asset is the sum of two independent
pieces, the systematic part $\boldsymbol\beta' \mathbf f$, whose distribution
comes from the factor generator, and the residual $e$, whose distribution is
chosen so that the asset as a whole has the moments the analyst wants.

**The four questions the simulator asks about one asset, and what it does with each answer.**

*Question 1. What does this asset load on?* For each factor you either give a
beta, give a correlation with the factor, say "estimate it", or say "nothing".
A beta you give is used as is. A correlation you give is turned into the beta
that produces it, given the other betas. "Estimate it" runs a regression of
the asset on those factors, after removing the part you gave; it needs a
history, and without one the simulator stops and asks for the values.
"Nothing" is a zero. If you say nothing at all about the asset, every factor
is "estimate it".

*Question 2. How volatile is the asset?* If you give the volatility, the
factors you chose already produce some of it,
$\boldsymbol\beta' \boldsymbol\Sigma_f \boldsymbol\beta$, and the residual
gets the rest: $\sigma_e^2 = v^2 - \boldsymbol\beta' \boldsymbol\Sigma_f \boldsymbol\beta$.
If the factors alone already exceed the volatility, the simulator stops and
tells you which betas are responsible. If you give nothing, the history gives
the *residual's* volatility, the standard deviation of the regression
residual, and the asset's volatility follows,
$v^2 = \boldsymbol\beta' \boldsymbol\Sigma_f \boldsymbol\beta + \sigma_e^2$:
it equals the sample's when the factor targets are the sample's, and it moves
with them when you state a view on a factor's volatility or on a correlation.

*Question 3. What shape does the asset have?* If you give the skewness and
kurtosis, the factors already give the asset some of each, and the residual
is given the rest, because cumulants of independent parts add:

$$
\kappa_{3,e} = s\, v^3 - \kappa_3(\boldsymbol\beta' \mathbf f), \qquad
\kappa_{4,e} = (k - 3)\, v^4 - \kappa_4(\boldsymbol\beta' \mathbf f),
$$

so the residual has skewness $\kappa_{3,e} / \sigma_e^3$ and excess kurtosis
$\kappa_{4,e} / \sigma_e^4$, and a Johnson SU is fitted to them. The
systematic cumulants are computed from the simulated factor months. If the
rest is a shape no distribution has (the Johnson SU's kurtosis has a floor
that rises with the skewness squared), the simulator stops and says how much
of the asset's shape the factors already carry: an asset whose variance is
mostly systematic has the factors' shape, and cannot be given another. If you
give nothing, the history gives the *residual's* shape, the skewness and
kurtosis of the regression residual, and the asset's shape follows from the
factors' and the residual's. Without a history the residual is normal.

*Question 4. Does this asset have to be correlated with a named asset?* If
yes, the correlation is carried by the two residuals. The factors already
give the pair a correlation; the residual correlation supplies the difference,

$$
\rho^{e}_{ij} = \frac{\rho^{*}_{ij}\, s_i s_j - \boldsymbol\beta_i' \boldsymbol\Sigma_f \boldsymbol\beta_j}{\sigma_{e_i} \sigma_{e_j}},
$$

and it can only do so within a range: the factors' correlation plus or minus
$\sqrt{(1 - R_i^2)(1 - R_j^2)}$, the geometric mean of what the factors leave
unexplained in each asset. Outside the range the simulator stops and prints
the range. Several pairs must form a positive-semidefinite residual
correlation matrix.

*Last, the mean.* If you give it, the alpha is set so that alpha plus the
factors' contribution equals it, $\alpha = m - \boldsymbol\beta' \boldsymbol\mu_f$.
If you give nothing, the history gives the alpha, the intercept of the
regression, and the mean follows the factor means: it equals the sample's
when the factor targets are the sample's, and a view on a factor's mean moves
it by the beta times the change. The mean comes last because nothing else
depends on it.

**One principle behind the four answers.** An empty cell means "what the
history says about the model's own quantity": the betas, the alpha, the
residual's volatility and shape. It never means "the sample moment of the
asset as a target", because a target would be absorbed by the alpha or the
residual and a view on a factor would then reach no asset. Given cells are
targets; empty cells are estimates.

The questions are asked in this order because each answer needs the ones
before: the residual variance needs the betas, the residual shape needs the
residual variance, the pair range needs the residual variances. The implied
covariance of the assets is $\mathbf B \boldsymbol\Sigma_f \mathbf B' + \mathbf D$,
with $\mathbf D$ the residual covariance, diagonal except where question 4
filled an entry. The specification report prints, for every asset, the four
answers and any flag.

**This notebook's case now.** A history for every asset and no other
knowledge: every factor "estimate it", alpha and residual from the
regression, no pairs. The regression is ordinary least squares with Newey-West standard
errors. The code prints the betas of seven assets, the $R^2$ quantiles across
the 68, and the specification report for the seven.
""")
code(r'''
show = ["SPY", "XLF", "EWZ", "TLT", "HYG", "GLD", "XLU"]
display(sim.model.report.loc[show, ["alpha"] + sim.model.factors + ["R2", "resid vol"]].round(3))
print("R2 across the 68 assets, quantiles:"); display(sim.model.r2.quantile([0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1]).round(2).to_frame("R2").T)
sim.report.loc[show]
''')

specification report every asset reads `history` with seven `fit` betas, and

between $-0.2$ and 1.6, kurtosis between 3.1 and 15 for HYG, whose 2008

## Appendix: method notes on the dependence of near-orthogonal factors

*Internal diagnostics; the analyst can skip them.* Three facts behind the
paragraph of step 2a.

**The symmetry test on the factor pairs.** For each first-tree pair, the
difference between the mean exceedance correlation on the left and on the
right of the profile, on the copula scale, with its 90 percent bootstrap
interval (notebook 02). A pair is asymmetric when the interval excludes zero.
""")
code(r'''
sim.market.classification.round(3)

md(r"""
**The exceedance profiles of the factors, on two scales.** Each factor
against Equity DM: the history in black, the C-vine's simulated months in
blue, the Fleishman market on the same targets in grey. The top row is on the
returns; the bottom row on normal scores, $\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{rank}/(n+1))$,
where the marginals are removed and only the copula remains. On the returns
the history's profiles lean left where the factor is correlated with equity;
part of that slope is Equity DM's own skewness, which makes its conditional
variance larger in its bad months whatever the copula, and both generators
show it. On the copula scale the pairs are close to flat, which is what the
symmetry test measured.
""")
code(r'''
normal_scores = lambda v: pd.Series(stats.norm.ppf(stats.rankdata(v) / (len(v) + 1)), index=getattr(v, "index", None))
base = "Equity DM"; others_f = [f for f in F.columns if f != base]; zg = np.round(np.arange(-1, 1.0001, 0.05), 3)
Fcv, Ffl = sim.pilot, sim_fl.pilot

    for j, f in enumerate(others_f):
        for row, (label, tf) in enumerate([("returns", lambda v: v), ("normal scores (copula scale)", normal_scores)]):
            ax = axes[row, j]
            for G, col, name in [(F, "black", "history"), (Ffl, "tab:grey", "Fleishman"), (Fcv, "tab:blue", "C-vine")]:
                ax.plot(zg, exceedance_curve(tf(G[base]), tf(G[f]), zg).values, color=col, lw=2 if name == "history" else 1.4, label=name)
            ax.axvline(0, color="grey", lw=0.6, ls=":"); ax.set_title(f"{f} vs {base}\n{label}", fontsize=9); ax.set_xlabel(f"threshold on {base} (sd)", fontsize=8)
    axes[0, 0].set_ylabel("exceedance correlation"); axes[1, 0].set_ylabel("exceedance correlation"); axes[0, 0].legend(fontsize=8)
    plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
''')
md(r"""
**How far apart the families are, by correlation.** For each first-tree pair,
every family of the catalog (Gaussian, Student t, Clayton and Gumbel in the
two rotations matching the sign of the dependence) is fitted by maximum
likelihood to the pair's pseudo-observations, and the exceedance profile of
each fitted copula is simulated on the copula scale. The table gives the pair's
correlation, the BIC gap per observation between the best and the worst
family, and the largest distance between any two families' profiles. When the
correlation is small the families are the same object: the choice cannot be
identified from the data and has no consequence for the scenarios. Where the
correlation is large the families differ, and the selection matters and is
testable.
""")
code(r'''

from cvinemarketgen.copulas import CopulaTools
_ct = CopulaTools(); zz = np.array([-1.0, -0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0])
def pobs(x, y): return np.column_stack([stats.rankdata(x) / (len(x) + 1), stats.rankdata(y) / (len(y) + 1)])
def profile(cop, n=100000):
    s = cop.simulate(n, seeds=[1, 2, 3, 4, 5]); return exceedance_curve(stats.norm.ppf(s[:, 0]), stats.norm.ppf(s[:, 1]), zz).values
