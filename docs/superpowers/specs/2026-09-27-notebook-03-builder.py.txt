sec("s1", "1. The problem", "")
md(r"""
You manage sixty-eight funds and you need scenarios: synthetic months of
returns, one column per fund, that feed an optimizer or a risk engine.

Three things stop you describing them one by one.

1. Sixty-eight assets have 2,278 correlations. From 230 months these cannot
   be estimated one at a time; the matrix is mostly noise.
2. Some assets have no history. A private credit fund, a new product: you
   know what it earns, how much it moves, and what it depends on, and that is
   all.
3. Your views are about drivers, not funds. "Equities return 5 percent",
   "bonds diversify again". Written on sixty-eight funds one by one, a view
   contradicts itself.
""")

sec("s2", "2. How it works", "")
md(r"""
The simulator draws a few common drivers, the factors, and maps each
simulated factor month to every asset:

$$
r_j = \alpha_j + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{jk}\, f_k + e_j .
$$

The factors are drawn together by the generator of notebook 02, which
reproduces their moments, their correlations and their tail dependence. The
betas say how much of each factor an asset carries. The residual $e_j$ is
what is left of the asset once the factors are removed.

Eight factors have 28 correlations instead of 2,278. An asset without a
history is its exposures plus a residual. A view is stated on a factor and
reaches every asset through its betas.

Four steps: put what you know in a file, fit, check, use.
""")

from cvinemarketgen import show_workbook, show_sheet, show_change, sheet_view    # a workbook drawn as its sheets

sec("s3", "3. The workbook", "Everything you know about your universe goes in one file.")
md(r"""
Six sheets.

| Sheet | What it holds |
|---|---|
| `assets` | what to simulate: a ticker, its tag, and any moment you impose |
| `tags` | the a-priori table: one row per asset class, `fit` where the class may load on a factor |
| `exposures` | one asset on one factor, as a beta or as a correlation. It overrides the tag |
| `pairs` | a correlation between two assets |
| `factors` | a factor's mean, volatility, skewness, kurtosis |
| `factor_corr` | a correlation between two factors |

**A filled cell is a target. An empty cell is estimated from the history**,
and from a fallback when there is none: skewness 0 and kurtosis 3. For an
asset, estimated means the model's own quantity, the beta, the alpha and the
residual, not the asset's moment. That is what lets a view on a factor reach
it. An asset or factor with no history must have a mean and a volatility.

Here is the file this notebook reads.
""")

spec = MarketSpec.read(os.path.join(INPUTS, "universe_full.xlsx"))
show_workbook(spec, max_rows=5)
''')

TLT, a shape on GLD, and both for the two funds with no history. The drawing
keeps each sheet's own row numbers and shows the rows that hold something. The
other sixty-five assets are a ticker and a tag, and every moment of theirs
comes from their history. The `tags` sheet carries the exposures of the whole
universe: sixteen classes, a `fit` wherever the class may load on a factor.""")

tickers = [t for t in spec.assets["ticker"] if t not in ("Private credit", "Multi-strategy")]

idx = R.index.intersection(F.index); R, F = R.loc[idx], F.loc[idx]
print(f"assets {R.shape[1]} x {R.shape[0]} months | factors {F.shape[1]} x {F.shape[0]} | {idx.min()} to {idx.max()}")
''')

sec("s5", "5. Fit", "One call turns the file and the histories into a simulator.")
md(r"""
The simulator asks four questions about each asset and fills in what the
workbook leaves out.

| | Question | Cell filled | Cell empty |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What does it load on? | the beta, or the correlation turned into a beta | the beta is regressed |
| 2 | How volatile is it? | the residual takes the rest of the variance | the residual is the history's |
| 3 | What shape has it? | the residual takes the rest of the cumulants | the residual keeps its shape |
| 4 | Tied to another asset? | the two residuals carry the difference | the residuals are independent |
| | The mean | the alpha absorbs it | the alpha is the history's |

In that order: the residual's scale needs the betas, its shape needs the
scale, and a pair's room needs both.

$$
\sigma_e^2 = v^2 - \boldsymbol\beta' \boldsymbol\Sigma_f \boldsymbol\beta, \qquad
\kappa_{3,e} = s\,v^3 - \kappa_3(\boldsymbol\beta' \mathbf f), \qquad
\alpha = m - \boldsymbol\beta' \boldsymbol\mu_f
$$

Three things are refused: a volatility below what the betas impose, a shape
the residual cannot carry, a pair correlation outside
$\rho^{\text{sys}}_{ij} \pm \sqrt{(1-R_i^2)(1-R_j^2)}$. Each refusal names its
bound. The methods note derives them.
""")
code(r'''
t0 = time.time(); sim = FactorMarket(R, F, spec, generator="cvine", central="Equity DM").fit()
print(f"fitted in {time.time() - t0:.0f} s: {len(sim.model.assets)} assets on {len(sim.targets.assets)} factors")
sim.market.edges[sim.market.edges["tree"] == 1]
''')
md(r"""
The report says, for every asset, where each number came from.
""")

show = ["SPY", "TLT", "GLD", "HYG", "Private credit", "Multi-strategy"]
sim.report.loc[show]
''')

of a beta, which is what the `corr` column counts, and the fit solved it into
a beta. Private credit has no history: three betas from its tag row, mean and
volatility from the file, shape from the normal fallback.""")

sec("s8", "8. The answer", "What the tail of your portfolio looks like.")
md(r"""
Two portfolios: equal weight over the sixty-eight listed funds, and 60/40,
sixty percent spread over the equity funds and forty over the bond funds.
Volatility, value at risk and expected shortfall at 5 percent, three ways:
simulated, in the sample, and under a normal.
""")
code(r'''
tag = spec.assets.set_index("ticker")["tag"]
eq = [t for t in tickers if tag[t] in ("equity_dm", "equity_dm_commodity", "financials", "banks", "rate_sensitive_equity", "small_cap", "equity_em", "equity_em_commodity")]
bd = [t for t in tickers if tag[t] in ("treasury", "aggregate_credit", "high_yield")]
w_eq = pd.Series(1.0 / len(tickers), index=tickers)
w_bal = pd.Series(0.0, index=tickers); w_bal[eq] = 0.6 / len(eq); w_bal[bd] = 0.4 / len(bd)
rows = {}
for name, w in [("equal weight, 68 funds", w_eq), ("60/40", w_bal)]:
    r_sim = (scen[tickers] @ w).values; r_hist = (R @ w).values
    for lab, r in [("history", r_hist), ("simulated", r_sim)]:
        v, e = var_es(r); rows[(name, lab)] = {"vol (%/month)": r.std() * 100, "skewness": stats.skew(r), "kurtosis": stats.kurtosis(r) + 3, "VaR 5% (%)": v * 100, "ES 5% (%)": e * 100}
    m, s = r_sim.mean(), r_sim.std()
    rows[(name, "normal, same mean and vol")] = {"vol (%/month)": s * 100, "skewness": 0.0, "kurtosis": 3.0,
                                                 "VaR 5% (%)": -(m + s * stats.norm.ppf(0.05)) * 100,
                                                 "ES 5% (%)": -(m - s * stats.norm.pdf(stats.norm.ppf(0.05)) / 0.05) * 100}
pd.DataFrame(rows).T.round(2)
''')
md("""Both simulated portfolios are slightly quieter than the sample, 3.88 percent a
month against 4.12 for equal weight, and their shape is the sample's:
skewness -0.62 against -0.63, kurtosis 4.76 against 5.30. A normal with the
same mean and volatility puts the expected shortfall 1.4 points too low on
equal weight and 1.1 too low on 60/40. That gap is what the factor shapes and
the residual copula add to the answer.""")

sec("s9", "9. What this misses, and what comes next", "")
md(r"""
The scenarios are one-period draws with no order in time, and a factor's
history has order: large months arrive together. Paths made of independent
months understate the risk over a horizon.

**Notebook 04** takes one factor's history as a time series and builds the
object that produces paths. **Notebook 05** puts the two together: a filter
per factor, their errors drawn jointly from the vine, and the assets of this
notebook carried along.

To change one thing at a time, see the recipes in `examples/recipes/`. For
the derivations, the feasible shapes, the correlation bounds, the completion
of a factor's correlations, and for why the copula layer is quiet on
near-orthogonal factors, see the methods note.
""")
