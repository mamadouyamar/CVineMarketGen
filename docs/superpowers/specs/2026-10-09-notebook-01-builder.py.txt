# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 1. the problem
sec('s1', '1. The problem', 'One sample, and every number you need rests on it.')
md(r"""
You have a few hundred monthly returns for a handful of assets. That is one
sample, and it is the only one there is. You need more data that looks like
it: to size a portfolio's risk, to test a rule, to answer a question about
months that did not happen.

Three things make the sample too small.

1. A 5 percent quantile on 261 months rests on 13 observations. Add or drop
   two months and the number moves.
2. A stress you want to ask about may not be in the sample at all.
3. You cannot vary anything. The sample has one set of correlations, and no
   way to ask what a different one would cost.
""")

| 2 | **Fit** | a generator that reproduces them |
| 3 | **Check** | the generator's draw against the targets |
| 4 | **Use** | draw what you need, or change a target and redraw |

Here the targets are the means, volatilities, skewnesses, kurtoses and

""")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 4. targets
sec('s4', '4. Targets', 'The numbers the synthetic data must reproduce.')
md(r"""
From $n$ observations $r_{i,1}, \dots, r_{i,n}$ of asset $i$, the targets are
the sample moments

$$
\mu_i = \frac{1}{n} \sum_t r_{i,t}, \qquad
\sigma_i^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_t (r_{i,t} - \mu_i)^2, \qquad
\gamma_{1,i} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_t (r_{i,t} - \mu_i)^3}{\hat s_i^3}, \qquad
\gamma_{2,i} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_t (r_{i,t} - \mu_i)^4}{\hat s_i^4},
$$

with $\hat s_i$ the biased standard deviation, and the Pearson correlation
matrix $\Sigma^*$ with entries $\Sigma^*_{ik} = \mathrm{Corr}(r_i, r_k)$.

Four numbers per asset and one per pair, so $4N + N(N-1)/2$ targets, 39 here.
The kurtosis is raw: the normal has $\gamma_2 = 3$, a value above 3 means
fatter tails, a negative skewness means the large moves are falls.
""")
md('`Targets.from_history` computes them and keeps the history for the checks below.')
cd("""
t = Targets.from_history(X)
print(t)
t.moments.round(3)
""")
md(r"""
Equity has negative skewness and kurtosis above 4. The Treasuries have
positive skewness. Gold is close to normal.

And the correlations.
""")
cd("""
t.corr.round(2)
""")
md('The two Treasury funds move together at 0.92 and are nearly uncorrelated with equity.')

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 5. fit
sec('s5', '5. Fit', 'A cubic of a normal per asset, the dependence in the normals.')
md(r"""
The simplest generator with these targets is Fleishman's (1978). Each asset is
a cubic polynomial of a standard normal,

$$
X_i = a_i + b_i Z_i + c_i Z_i^2 + d_i Z_i^3, \qquad Z_i \sim N(0, 1).
$$

The moments of a polynomial of a normal are polynomials of its coefficients,
so matching mean 0, variance 1 and the target skewness and kurtosis is three
equations in $(b_i, c_i, d_i)$, with $a_i = -c_i$, solved numerically. The
mean and volatility are applied afterwards, $r_i = \mu_i + \sigma_i X_i$. A
solution exists when $\gamma_{2,i} - 3 \ge \gamma_{1,i}^2 - 2$, which every
return series in practice satisfies.
""")
md(r"""
The dependence goes in the normals. If $(Z_i, Z_k)$ have correlation
$\rho_Z$, the cubics have correlation

$$
\mathrm{Corr}(X_i, X_k) = \rho_Z \,(b_i b_k + 3 b_i d_k + 3 d_i b_k + 9 d_i d_k) + \rho_Z^2\, (2 c_i c_k) + \rho_Z^3\, (6 d_i d_k),
$$

(Vale and Maurelli, 1983). For each pair the *intermediate* correlation
$\rho_Z$ is the root of that cubic which makes $\mathrm{Corr}(X_i, X_k)$ equal
the target $\Sigma^*_{ik}$. The matrix of intermediate correlations is made
positive definite if needed, and the normals are drawn from it by a Cholesky
factor.

The fitted polynomial per asset.
""")
cd("""
fm = FleishmanMarket(t).fit()
print(fm)
fm.coefficients.round(4)
""")
md('How far the normals had to move to deliver the targets.')
cd("""
print("intermediate correlations of the normals minus the target correlations (max abs):", round(float((fm.intermediate_corr - t.corr).abs().values.max()), 4))
""")
md('Four thousandths: the polynomials are close to linear here, so the two matrices nearly coincide.')

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6. check

`simulate(n)` draws $n$ joint observations, and the draw is compared with
every target: four sample moments per asset, one correlation per pair.

The generator refits its coefficients on the draw and accepts it only when
every difference is inside tolerance, redrawing otherwise. The tolerances are
$2 \times 10^{-4}$ on means and volatilities, $5 \times 10^{-2}$ on skewness
and kurtosis, $0.02$ on correlations.

Run this check every time a market is fitted. It says whether the synthetic
data is what it was asked to be.
""")

""")

correlation and its elongated shape.
""")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7. use
sec('s7', '7. Use', 'A portfolio tail on 25,000 months, and why it is short.')
md(r"""
From here the synthetic data replaces the history as the input to any
calculation: a portfolio's risk, the performance of a rule, a stress. The gain
is size. The history's 5 percent quantile rests on 13 months; the synthetic
one rests on 1,250.
""")

sub('s7-1', '7.1 The equity portfolio')
md(r"""
Equal weights in U.S. large caps, U.S. small caps and developed markets
outside the U.S. (SPY, IWM, EFA): diversification across markets, whose value
is decided in bad months.

A portfolio is a fixed vector of weights $\mathbf w$ and its return on each
observation is $r_p = \mathbf w' \mathbf r$. Its monthly 5 percent value at
risk and expected shortfall are

$$
\mathrm{VaR}_{5\%} = -q_{0.05}(r_p), \qquad \mathrm{ES}_{5\%} = -\mathbb E\big[r_p \mid r_p \le q_{0.05}(r_p)\big],
$$

minus the 5 percent quantile, and minus the mean of the returns below it.

Both, three ways: simulated, in the sample, and under a normal.
""")
keep(15)
md(r"""
The normal gives the smallest tail, because it ignores the skewness and
kurtosis of the assets. The synthetic data gives a larger one, since its
marginals carry them. The history gives the largest, above the synthetic
value, although the generator reproduced every target.
""")

sub('s7-2', '7.2 Why the synthetic tail is short')
md(r"""
The Cornish-Fisher expansion writes a quantile as a function of the first four
moments: with $z = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$, skewness $\gamma_1$ and excess kurtosis
$\kappa = \gamma_2 - 3$,

$$
q_\alpha \approx \mu + \sigma \left[ z + \frac{z^2 - 1}{6}\gamma_1 + \frac{z^3 - 3z}{24}\kappa - \frac{2z^3 - 5z}{36}\gamma_1^2 \right].
$$

So if the portfolio's four moments were matched, its VaR would be. The
portfolio's four moments, its empirical VaR and its Cornish-Fisher VaR.
""")
cd(_SPLIT17A := '''
def cf_var(r, alpha=0.05):
    m, s, g1, k = r.mean(), r.std(), stats.skew(r), stats.kurtosis(r)                       # k: excess kurtosis
    z = stats.norm.ppf(alpha); zc = z + (z**2 - 1) * g1 / 6 + (z**3 - 3 * z) * k / 24 - (2 * z**3 - 5 * z) * g1**2 / 36
    return -(m + s * zc)
rows = {}
for name, r in [("history", rp_hist.values), ("synthetic (Fleishman)", rp_sim.values)]:
    rows[name] = {"mean (%)": r.mean() * 100, "vol (%)": r.std() * 100, "skewness": stats.skew(r), "kurtosis": stats.kurtosis(r) + 3,
                  "VaR 5%, empirical (%)": var_es(r)[0] * 100, "VaR 5%, Cornish-Fisher (%)": cf_var(r) * 100}
pd.DataFrame(rows).T.round(2)
''')
md(r"""
The mean and volatility agree, the skewness and kurtosis do not: $-0.62$ and
4.7 against $-0.44$ and 4.2. Cornish-Fisher splits the gap in two. On the
synthetic data it reproduces the empirical VaR, 7.30 against 7.31. On the
history it gives 7.47 against the empirical 8.22, so the moments account for
about 0.2 of VaR and the remaining 0.75 is the sampling noise of a quantile on
13 months. The expected shortfall, which averages those 13 months instead of
picking one, agrees to 0.2 as well.
""")
md(r"""
So the real gap sits in the portfolio's third and fourth moments. Why are they
not matched, when each asset's four are?

Because a portfolio's skewness and kurtosis depend on how the assets move
*together in the tails*, and no target described that. Each asset's moments
describe it alone, and a correlation is one number per pair, an average over
the whole distribution.

The sharper question: how correlated are two equity markets when one of them
falls? For a pair $(x, y)$ with $x$ standardized and a threshold $z$ in
standard deviations, the exceedance correlation is

$$
\hat\rho(z) = \begin{cases} \mathrm{Corr}(x, y \mid x < z) & z < 0, \\ \mathrm{Corr}(x, y \mid x \ge z) & z \ge 0, \end{cases}
$$

the Pearson correlation on the observations where $x$ is below $z$, its bad
months, or above it, its good ones.

At $z = -1, 0, +1$, for SPY against EFA and IWM, with each asset's own tail beside it.
""")
cd('''
rows = {}
for name, D in [("history", X), ("synthetic (Fleishman)", S)]:
    e = exceedance_curve(D["SPY"], D["EFA"], [-1.0, 0.0, 1.0]); i = exceedance_curve(D["SPY"], D["IWM"], [-1.0, 0.0, 1.0])
    rows[name] = {"corr(SPY, EFA | SPY < -1 sd)": e.iloc[0], "corr(SPY, EFA | SPY >= 0)": e.iloc[1], "corr(SPY, EFA | SPY >= +1 sd)": e.iloc[2],
                  "corr(SPY, IWM | SPY < -1 sd)": i.iloc[0], "corr(SPY, IWM | SPY >= +1 sd)": i.iloc[2],
                  "SPY VaR 5% (%)": var_es(D["SPY"].values)[0] * 100, "EFA VaR 5% (%)": var_es(D["EFA"].values)[0] * 100}
pd.DataFrame(rows).T.round(2)
''')
md(r"""
In the history SPY correlates 0.81 with EFA in SPY's bad months and 0.34 in
its good ones: diversification across markets works when it is not needed and
fails when it is. In the synthetic data the profile is flat at the target, 0.62
on both sides.

The generator has the right correlation on average and the wrong one in the
tail, and an equal-weight equity portfolio inherits the difference as missing
skewness and kurtosis. Each asset's own tail, in the last two columns, is
reproduced. The joint tail is not.
""")

sub('s7-3', '7.3 A second portfolio: equity and bonds')
md(r"""
Most portfolios are not equity only. A 60/40, 60 percent SPY and 40 percent
TLT, relies on Treasuries to offset equity's bad months; in the sample the two
are almost uncorrelated, $-0.09$.

Its tail and its shape, the same three ways.
""")
cd('''
w2 = pd.Series({"SPY": 0.6, "TLT": 0.4}).reindex(X.columns).fillna(0.0)
rp2_sim, rp2_hist = (S @ w2).values, (X @ w2).values
mu2, sd2 = rp2_hist.mean(), rp2_hist.std()
rows = {"synthetic (Fleishman)": var_es(rp2_sim) + (stats.skew(rp2_sim), stats.kurtosis(rp2_sim) + 3),
        "history (261 months)": var_es(rp2_hist) + (stats.skew(rp2_hist), stats.kurtosis(rp2_hist) + 3),
        "normal, same mean and vol": (-(mu2 + sd2 * stats.norm.ppf(0.05)), -(mu2 - sd2 * stats.norm.pdf(stats.norm.ppf(0.05)) / 0.05), 0.0, 3.0)}
tab = pd.DataFrame(rows, index=["VaR 5% (%)", "ES 5% (%)", "skewness", "kurtosis"]).T
tab[["VaR 5% (%)", "ES 5% (%)"]] *= 100
tab.round(2)
''')
md(r"""
Again the mean and volatility agree and the skewness and kurtosis do not,
$-0.68$ and 5.0 against $-0.31$ and 3.8, so the same gap in the tail. The
cause, though, is different.
""")
md(r"""
Here there is no asymmetry to reproduce. What the history has and the
synthetic data has not is the *co-kurtosis*

$$
\mathbb E[s^2 b^2], \qquad s = \frac{r_{SPY} - \mu_{SPY}}{\sigma_{SPY}},\ \ b = \frac{r_{TLT} - \mu_{TLT}}{\sigma_{TLT}},
$$

the average product of the squared standardized returns, which equals
$1 + 2\rho^2$ when the sizes of the two moves are independent.

The exceedance correlations, TLT's own tail, and that co-kurtosis against its
independent-sizes value.
""")
cd('''
z_ = lambda v: (v - v.mean()) / v.std()                     # standardize
rows = {}
for name, D in [("history", X), ("synthetic (Fleishman)", S)]:
    e = exceedance_curve(D["SPY"], D["TLT"], [-1.0, 0.0, 1.0]); s_, b_ = z_(D["SPY"]), z_(D["TLT"]); rho = np.corrcoef(s_, b_)[0, 1]
    rows[name] = {"corr(SPY, TLT | SPY < -1 sd)": e.iloc[0], "corr(SPY, TLT | SPY >= 0)": e.iloc[1], "corr(SPY, TLT | SPY >= +1 sd)": e.iloc[2],
                  "TLT VaR 5% (%)": var_es(D["TLT"].values)[0] * 100, "1 + 2 rho^2": 1 + 2 * rho**2, "co-kurtosis E[s^2 b^2]": np.mean(s_**2 * b_**2)}
pd.DataFrame(rows).T.round(2)
''')
md(r"""
The exceedance correlation is near zero on both sides, in the history and in
the synthetic data alike: no asymmetry here. The co-kurtosis is 1.75 in the
history against the independent-sizes value of 1.01. Large months of equity and
large months of Treasuries coincide, in either direction, as in 2008, 2020 and
2022, so the portfolio's variance is not the same every month and its tail is
fatter than its correlation says. The synthetic data has the Gaussian value.

Two portfolios, two properties of the joint tail that moments and correlations
do not describe. For equity markets, dependence stronger in bad months than in
good ones. For equity and bonds, large moves that coincide with no direction.
Notebook 02 makes both a target.
""")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 8. views
sec('s8', '8. Views', 'Change one target, read the consequence.')
md(r"""
The targets are inputs, not just statistics. A view is a change to one of them
with everything else kept.
""")

sub('s8-1', '8.1 The correlations between equity markets')
md(r"""
The three equity markets correlate 0.77 to 0.89 in the sample. The view sets
all three to 0.50, the level of the 1990s, and keeps every other target: the
same means, volatilities, skewnesses and kurtoses, the same correlations with
bonds and gold.

Nothing should happen to any single asset, by construction, only to the way
they add up. For $N$ assets with weights $w_i$,

$$
\sigma_p^2 = \sum_i w_i^2 \sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i \ne k} w_i w_k\, \rho_{ik}\, \sigma_i \sigma_k ,
$$

so lowering the three $\rho_{ik}$ from about 0.84 to 0.50 removes a third of
the second sum.

Everything below is simulated under each set of targets, except the implied
portfolio volatility, which is the check.
""")

md(r"""
The assets' own volatilities are unchanged and the portfolio's matches the
implied value. Its monthly volatility falls from 4.7 to 4.0 percent, 13
percent less risk for the same assets, and the VaR and expected shortfall fall
with it.
""")

SPY's volatility target goes to 6 percent a month, 21 percent a year, against
the sample's 4.3 percent, a rise of 40 percent. The other 38 targets are
unchanged.

In the variance formula above $\sigma_{SPY}$ enters its own term and its two
cross terms, with a weight of one third. The portfolio volatility should rise
by about 12 percent, far less than SPY's 40.

The two sets of targets, side by side.
""")

It rises from 4.7 to 5.2 percent a month, and the simulated value matches the

""")
