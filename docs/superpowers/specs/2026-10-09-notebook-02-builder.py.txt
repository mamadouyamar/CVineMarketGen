""")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 4
sec('s4', '4. Marginals and copula', 'Sklar, and the dependence seen by ranking.')
md(r"""
Sklar's theorem: any joint distribution $F$ of $N$ variables with continuous
marginals $F_1, \dots, F_N$ can be written

$$
F(x_1, \dots, x_N) = C\big(F_1(x_1), \dots, F_N(x_N)\big),
$$

where $C$, the copula, is a distribution on $[0,1]^N$ with uniform marginals,
and it is unique.

The variables $U_i = F_i(X_i)$ are uniform whatever $F_i$ is, so $C$ is the
dependence with the marginals removed. The skewness and kurtosis of notebook
01 live in the $F_i$; the way the assets move together lives in $C$.
""")
md(r"""
In notebook 01 the marginals were Fleishman cubics and the copula was
Gaussian, without anyone choosing it: mixing assets through correlated normals
imposes it. A Gaussian copula is fixed by its correlations and has no tails.
Given that one variable is in its far tail, the other is no more likely to be
in its own than the correlation implies.

The copula of a pair is seen by ranking: $\hat U_{i,t} = \mathrm{rank}(r_{i,t}) / (n + 1)$
puts each return at its position in its own sample.

The ranks of SPY against EFA, beside a Gaussian copula with the same correlation.
""")
cd('''
U = pd.DataFrame({c: stats.rankdata(X[c]) / (len(X) + 1) for c in X.columns}, index=X.index)     # empirical copula (pseudo-observations)
rho = np.corrcoef(stats.norm.ppf(U["SPY"]), stats.norm.ppf(U["EFA"]))[0, 1]
G = pv.Bicop(pv.BicopFamily.gaussian, 0, parameters=np.array([[rho]])).simulate(len(X), seeds=[1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.6))
axes[0].scatter(U["SPY"], U["EFA"], s=8); axes[0].set_title(f"history: ranks of SPY and EFA ({len(X)} months)")
axes[1].scatter(G[:, 0], G[:, 1], s=8, color="tab:grey"); axes[1].set_title(f"Gaussian copula, same correlation ({rho:.2f})")
for ax in axes: ax.set_xlabel("SPY rank"); ax.set_ylabel("EFA rank"); ax.set_aspect("equal")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
''')
md('Read the corners, and count them.')
cd('''
corner = lambda u, v, q: (np.mean((u <= q) & (v <= q)) / q, np.mean((u > 1 - q) & (v > 1 - q)) / q)
print("share of months in the lower-left corner (both below their 10% quantile) divided by 10%, and the same upper-right:")
print("  history %.2f / %.2f   Gaussian copula %.2f / %.2f" % (*corner(U["SPY"].values, U["EFA"].values, 0.10), *corner(G[:, 0], G[:, 1], 0.10)))
''')
md(r"""
The history piles more mass into the lower-left corner, both assets in their
worst months, than into the upper-right. The Gaussian draw is thinner on the
left and the same on the right.

Its own two corners differ too, and that is sampling noise: the copula which
produced it is symmetric by construction, and only twenty-six months land in
each corner. Section 6 is about exactly that.
""")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 5
sec('s5', '5. Tail dependence', 'With a direction, and without one.')
sub('s5-1', '5.1 With a direction')
md(r"""
The corner counts estimate the tail-dependence coefficients

$$
\lambda_L = \lim_{q \to 0} \Pr\big(U_2 \le q \mid U_1 \le q\big) = \lim_{q \to 0} \frac{C(q, q)}{q}, \qquad
\lambda_U = \lim_{q \to 1} \Pr\big(U_2 > q \mid U_1 > q\big) = \lim_{q \to 1} \frac{1 - 2q + C(q, q)}{1 - q},
$$

the probability that one variable is in its extreme tail given that the other
is. For the Gaussian copula both are zero, whatever the correlation.
""")
md(r"""
Two one-parameter families put mass in one corner. Clayton,
$C(u, v) = (u^{-\theta} + v^{-\theta} - 1)^{-1/\theta}$, has
$\lambda_L = 2^{-1/\theta}$ and $\lambda_U = 0$. Gumbel,
$C(u, v) = \exp\{-[(-\ln u)^\theta + (-\ln v)^\theta]^{1/\theta}\}$, has
$\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{1/\theta}$ and $\lambda_L = 0$.

Rotating by 180 degrees, $C_{180}(u, v) = u + v - 1 + C(1 - u, 1 - v)$, swaps
the corners; 90 and 270 degrees turn positive dependence into negative. So the
catalog covers each corner with either sign.

The exceedance profile of notebook 01 is the finite-sample view of the same
thing: a copula with $\lambda_L > 0$ and $\lambda_U = 0$ has a profile higher
on the left than on the right.

Each family fitted to the ranks of SPY and EFA by maximum likelihood.
""")
keep(8)
md(r"""
The Gumbel rotated by 180 degrees wins by 19 BIC units over the Gaussian, and
the two upper-tail families lose by 38 and 102. That is the asymmetry of the
scatter above, read as a likelihood.

Clayton is the caution. It has the largest lower-tail coefficient of all six
and still sits 4 units worse than the Gaussian: a family is chosen on its whole
shape, not on the corner it can reach.
""")

sub('s5-2', '5.2 Without a direction')
md(r"""
Equity and Treasuries showed no asymmetry in notebook 01, and a co-kurtosis of
1.75 against the Gaussian's 1.02. Their large moves coincide, in either
direction.

The family for that is the Student t copula, the copula of a bivariate $t$
with correlation $\rho$ and $\nu$ degrees of freedom. It is symmetric, and
both coefficients equal

$$
\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 2\, t_{\nu + 1}\!\left( -\sqrt{\frac{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)}{1 + \rho}} \right),
$$

positive even at $\rho = 0$, falling to the Gaussian's zero as $\nu \to \infty$.

It has two parameters where the others have one. $\rho$ plays the role of the
correlation and is calibrated to the target like any other family's parameter;
$\nu$ is estimated from the history and sets how much the sizes of the moves
co-move.

Both fitted to SPY and TLT, each simulated with Johnson SU marginals to show
its co-kurtosis.
""")
keep(10)
md(r"""
The Student t wins by 12 BIC units, and its co-kurtosis overshoots the
history's where the Gaussian falls far short.

So the family carries the property, and carries too much of it. The pair's
correlation is almost zero, so the degrees of freedom are estimated on the
sizes of the moves alone, and 261 months put them low.
""")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6
sec('s6', '6. Selecting a family from the data', 'The noise, the scale, and the test.')
md(r"""
The paper's rule (Algorithm 3, Section 4.5.4) reads the exceedance profile on
thresholds from $-0.5$ to $0.5$ standard deviations. It then classifies the pair by
four characteristics.

| | Set when |
|---|---|
| $\ell = 1$ | the profile is higher on the left |
| $u = 1$ | the profile is higher on the right |
| $s$ | the sign of the target correlation |
| $m = -1$ | the profile changes sign |

The families whose tail coefficients match the class are fitted and the lowest
BIC is kept. A sign-changing pair gets a two-component mixture
$w\,C_1 + (1 - w)\,C_2$.
""")

sub('s6-1', '6.1 The noise problem')
md(r"""
The profile is estimated, and on 261 months it is noisy. The conditional
correlation at $z = -1$ uses the months where the central asset is below minus
one standard deviation, about 40 of them, with a standard error near 0.15.

So measure the consequence on pairs simulated from a Gaussian copula, which
has no asymmetry at all.

Forty of them per correlation, at the ETF sample size.
""")
keep(12)
md(r"""
Three quarters are classified asymmetric at either correlation, and at the
negative one four fifths are called sign-changing as well. These pairs have no
asymmetry at all, so the rule is reading noise as structure.

The last column is the remedy: with the test first, 88 percent get a Gaussian
or a Student t at both correlations.
""")

sub('s6-2', '6.2 The scale the profile is read on')
md(r"""
One thing changes from the paper. Its first tree read the profile on the
standardized returns, and a profile on returns slopes with the skewness of the
conditioning variable whatever the copula: a longer left tail means a larger
conditional variance there, hence a higher correlation. Marginal skewness was
being read as copula asymmetry.

The classification and the test below are computed on the *normal scores*
$\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{rank}/(n+1))$ of the two series, the copula scale the
deeper trees of the vine already used. Only the dependence is read.
""")

sub('s6-3', '6.3 The symmetry test')
md(r"""
The remedy for the noise is a test before the classification. The statistic is
the mean of the profile on the left minus its mean on the right,
$\bar\rho_L - \bar\rho_R$, on thresholds from $-1$ to $1$ standard deviations.
The classification's own window, $-0.5$ to $0.5$, gives the test too many
false asymmetries.

Its sampling distribution comes from resampling the observations with
replacement, 500 times. When zero lies inside the central 90 percent interval
the asymmetry is not distinguishable from noise, the pair is *symmetric*, and
the candidates are the Gaussian and the Student t by BIC. Otherwise the
classification applies as before.

The test on SPY against each other asset.
""")
keep(13)
md(r"""
The two equity pairs are asymmetric, their intervals clear of zero. SPY against
TLT and SPY against GLD are not: both statistics sit near zero inside an
interval about half a unit wide.

Without the test, section 6.1 says those last two would have been classified
asymmetric about three times in four.
""")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7
sec('s7', '7. Fit', 'The C-vine: a copula per pair, calibrated to the targets.')
md(r"""
With $N$ assets there are $N(N-1)/2$ pairs, and a set of pair copulas is not
automatically a valid joint copula. The C-vine makes it one. With asset 1 as
the central node the joint density factorises into $N - 1$ pair copulas of
asset 1 with each other asset, then pair copulas of the rest given asset 1,
and so on,

$$
c(u_1, \dots, u_N) = \prod_{k=1}^{N-1} \prod_{i=k+1}^{N} c_{ik \mid 1:k-1}\big(F(u_i \mid u_{1:k-1}),\, F(u_k \mid u_{1:k-1})\big),
$$

every factor a bivariate copula chosen and fitted on its own. The conditional
arguments come from the $h$-functions
$h(u \mid v; \theta) = \partial C(u, v; \theta) / \partial v$ of the previous
tree.
""")
md(r"""
The families are selected tree by tree as in section 6, with the symmetry test
on each pair. The parameters are then *calibrated* rather than left at their
history values: for $i = 2, \dots, N$ the parameters of asset $i$'s edges solve

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_i = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta_i} \sum_{k < i} \Big( \mathrm{Corr}\big(F_i^{-1}(u_i(\boldsymbol\theta_i)),\, F_k^{-1}(u_k)\big) - \Sigma^*_{ik} \Big)^2
$$

on a fixed set of simulated uniforms (Algorithm 4), so the output's Pearson
correlations are the targets while the families keep the tails.

The marginals $F_i^{-1}$ are Johnson SU quantiles fitted to the four moments,
the paper's choice in place of the Fleishman cubic. The acceptance rule is
notebook 01's with a correlation tolerance of 0.05. SPY is the central node.
""")
keep(15)
md('The test and the class of each first-tree pair, above; the family and calibrated parameters of every edge, below.')
keep(16)
md(r"""
The two equity pairs receive a Gumbel rotated by 180 degrees, lower-tail
dependence: developed markets and small caps fall with U.S. large caps more
than they rise with them. The Treasury pairs receive a Student t with few
degrees of freedom, sizes moving together with no direction. Gold gets a
Gaussian.

The deeper trees carry what is left once SPY is accounted for, most of it
weak, with a Gumbel between the two Treasury funds given the equities.
""")

ones, with a mild asymmetry the test found significant. The Gumbel 180
reproduces the left side and, having no upper tail, under-reproduces the
right. A two-sided family with a stronger left side is not in the catalog.
""")
